# Technical Summary — EDA Precision Lab (Week 06 Day 1)

*Standalone write-up for non-technical readers; the full analysis with code lives in `week6_day1_precision_lab.ipynb`.*

## What the dataset contains

A log of **600 students** across three class sections (A, B, C), recording five measurements each: study hours per week, sleep hours per night, attendance percentage, class section, and exam score. The dataset was generated by a known formula (`exam_score = 50 + 2.6*study_hours + 0.15*attendance + section_bonus_C + noise`), so every relationship in it is designed and verifiable.

## What we were testing

Three questions that matter in any educational dataset:

1. **Do students who study more actually score higher?** — and if so, how strongly, as a number rather than an impression.
2. **Does sleep matter for exam performance?** — and if it doesn't, can we say that honestly rather than quietly dropping the chart.
3. **Do the three class sections perform differently?** — and if one does, is the difference real or an artifact of chart design.

## What was found

| Finding | Evidence | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Study hours show a strong positive relationship with exam score | Pearson r = 0.689 (p = 8.1e-86); each additional study hour associates with ~2.1 more points | Interventions targeting study time would have data support; this is the strongest lever in the dataset |
| Sleep hours show no meaningful relationship with exam score | Pearson r = -0.022 (p = 0.60); the point cloud is flat | A sleep-focused intervention would have no support from this data — sleep is not a driver of scores here |
| Section C outscores A and B by ~4 points | Mean scores: A = 87.6, B = 87.0, C = 91.4; gap = 3.75 points | The +4 section-C bonus was hard-coded into the generator; the data confirms it surfaces clearly |

## How visualization choices affect conclusions

A single chart type mistake can change what a reader takes away:

- **Line chart on categories** (the trap): connecting section means with a line fabricates movement — the eye reads a "rise from B to C" as a trend, even though categories have no inherent order. The bar chart correctly shows three independent groups.
- **Arbitrary colors** (red/green/blue per section): implies severity or status that doesn't exist. A single hue with one accent on the outlier section communicates only what the data supports.
- **Layout collisions**: an annotation placed on top of a legend looks fine inline in a notebook but breaks in the saved file. Every chart was checked programmatically for element overlap before shipping.

## Verification discipline

Every number in the findings was generated by code, not typed by hand. A self-audit table independently recomputes all three headline numbers (two correlations, one section mean) using different methods (`np.corrcoef` instead of `scipy.pearsonr`; boolean-mask means instead of `groupby`) and asserts they match exactly. This catches the class of error where a finding states "r = 0.42" when the code actually produced 0.41.

## Honest limitations

- **Correlation is not causation.** r = 0.689 licenses an association between study hours and scores, not a causal mechanism. Motivated students may also attend more, sleep differently, or have other confounding traits.
- **Synthetic, ideal-shaped data.** No missing values, no outliers, balanced groups — the difficulty was deliberately placed on interpretation and presentation, and real datasets will be less forgiving.
- **Linear lens only.** Pearson r measures straight-line association; a strong curved relationship could hide behind a small r. Scatter plots remain the visual sanity check.
- **Dataset scope.** The sleep finding is "no measurable linear association in these 600 students," not "sleep never matters anywhere."
